Structure the Talk

Goals define structure

Universal structure(suitable for every talk ever)
Warm-up, alignment with audience
Context of the problem, observations
Problem description and why it is important
Alternative solutions: why they fail
Your solution: methodology, approach, algorithm, ...
Evidence it is working: tests, evaluations, usage statistics, feedback, ...
Analysis: answer to the typical objections, ablation studies, implications.

Technical Challenges

What is Technical Challenge?
Challenge = Solving uncertainty!
Exploration with no obvious answer
Iterating through failed approaches
Evaluating real trade-offs based on evidence
← not required to tell about all iterations!

Practice: Evaluate challenges
“We used React, FastAPI, Redis, K8s”
“We implemented 15 endpoints, 8 screens it was a lot of work”
“We'd never used K8s before, it was very hard to learn it”

Not bad, but not a challenge!
Listing technologies"We used React, FastAPI, Redis, K8s" 	→ that's a stack, not a challenge
Volume of work"We implemented 15 endpoints, 8 screens it was a lot of work"	→ volume is not a challenge!
Learning was hard"We'd never used K8s before, it was very hard to learn it"	→ signals inexperience, not a challenge

Design Slides

Avoid long essay on the slides!
Putting large blocks of text on presentation slides is a poor design pattern that undermines effective communication because slides are meant to support the speaker, not replace them; when overloaded with dense paragraphs, they distract the audience, split attention, and often go unread; moreover, this approach encourages passive reading instead of active listening, making the presentation dull and harder to follow. 
Take into consideration that overwriting slides are signals of a lack of preparation or failure to prioritize what really matters, therefore, please, consider key ideas to be distilled into short and concise bullet points or visuals, allowing the speaker to elaborate verbally.

Delivery

Team narration: Split by Arc, Not by Responsibility
BAD — "who did what":
“Hi, I'm Alex, I did the backend."
"Hi, I'm Bella, I did the ML." / ...→ context switches, no narrative thread
GOOD — story arc:
Alex: problem + value + demo

John: evaluation, summary
Together: Q&A

Q&A

Why Jury asks?
Typical Reasons:
Can't score a criterion yet — need more information
Genuinely curious about something interesting.
Want to share their experience to help your project.
Neither is an attack!Confronting or rejecting is always a bad strategy.
Q&A = opportunity to show depth you didn't have time to cover

Q&A Strategy
Figure out which criterion is being probed → answer to help them score it
You may change a question a bit:"We didn't try X, but we solved the same problem this way"
Be brief: long 3-min answer eats time for other questions
Leave hooks during the talk:"No time now — ask me and I'll show a backup slide"
Acknowledge and clarify discovered weakness:→ rejecting is a sign of non-professionalism
